GTA 5, Spider-Man, Silent Hill 2.
Their PS5 versions already boot on a PC, with a free emulator called KytyPS5.
And another project skips the emulator entirely.

But first, why is this even possible?
On the PS3, the processor was so different from a PC's that every line of code had to be translated.
The PS5 uses the same family of processor as your PC, Intel or AMD.
Same language.
So most of the game's code just runs, directly on your processor.
What's missing is the rest of the console.
A game spends its whole time asking the PS5 for things.
Save my progress. Play this sound. What is the controller doing?
On a PC, nobody answers.
And the game draws with tiny programs called shaders, written in the PS5's own graphics language.
Your graphics card can't read them.

KytyPS5 fixes that while you play.
It launches the game, and every time the game asks the PS5 for something, KytyPS5 answers instead, with its own copy of that function.
Every shader gets translated on the fly, into Vulkan, a language your graphics card speaks.
And it works. Big games already reach playable scenes.
Silent Hill 2 runs, at 6 frames per second.
There are still bugs, crashes, and very uneven performance.
Reaching a scene is not finishing the game.

AnyPS5 does that work before you play.
It converts the game itself into a normal Windows or Linux program, already wired to rewritten PS5 functions.
No emulator to open. You double-click the game, and it starts, like any app.
That's why AnyPS5 calls itself a porting tool, not an emulator.
For now, its official list has exactly one game.
Dreaming Sarah, a small 2D platformer, at 60 frames per second on a GTX 1050 Ti.

And it's speeding up.
At the end of July, KytyPS5 shipped 2 to 3 new versions a day.
Since mid-September, more than 7.
More than 80 people have worked on its code, and its last weekly report shows progress on about fifteen games, in one week.
